The architecture of the college player model is extremely similar to that of the pro one, so rather than repeating most of it, this section will focus on the differences between the two models.

The biggest difference between the two models is the timestep; while the pro model operates on a monthly tick, the college one only updates yearly. This was not a design choice; rather, this was do to not having access to monthly data. This ends up creating problems that the model is unable to account for, degrading its performance. The model is given the players' yearly stats as well as the average ranking of their opponents, but with all of the values put together it doesn't have a way to discriminate between a player who destroyed poor teams and struggled in conference play and a player who was solid throughout the entire year.

The other main difference is that the college model has far less data on a player's defensive skills than the pro model does. The college model is only given the player's listed position, while the pro model has the percentage of games for each position a player played at as well as an internal DRS-based defensive metric. This leads to the college model having little idea how good a player actually is defensively, other than that most college C will play C in pro ball. This results in a model that is far more likely to give top marks for C than it is for other positions.
